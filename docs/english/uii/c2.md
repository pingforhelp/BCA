# I have a dream

A.

# Q1 What is the status about the Negro even after several years of the Emancipation Proclamation
signed?
One hundred years after the Emancipation Proclamation, King notes that the Negro is still not
free. Instead, Black Americans are crippled by the "manacles of segregation and the chains of discrimination." They live in severe poverty on a "lonely island" surrounded by a vast ocean of
American material prosperity. They are socially and economically marginalized, languishing in the corners of American society. Ultimately, despite the historical promise of freedom from
slavery, the current reality for the Negro is a life of systemic oppression, effectively making them exiles in their own land.

# Q2 What was promised by the Constitution and the Declaration of Independence?

The Constitution and the Declaration of Independence served as a "promissory note" to every
American citizen. It was a guarantee that all men—black men as well as white men—would be granted the "unalienable rights" of life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness. King argues that America defaulted on this sacred obligation for its citizens of color, handing them a "bad check" marked "insufficient funds," rather than honoring the promise of equality and justice that the founding documents laid out.

# Q3 What does the speaker want to remind America?

King wants to remind America of the "fierce urgency of now." He emphasizes that this is not the time for the nation to engage in the luxury of cooling off or to take the tranquilizing drug of
"gradualism" (slow, delayed reform). He urges the country to realize that immediate, pressing
action is required to make the promises of democracy a reality. He calls for the nation to rise immediately from the dark and desolate valley of segregation to the sunlit path of racial justice.

# Q4 Why can’t the nation overlook the urgency of the moment?

The nation cannot overlook the urgency of the moment because the legitimate discontent of Black
Americans will not pass until true equality is achieved. King warns that there will be neither rest
nor tranquility in America until the Negro is granted their full citizenship rights. The "whirlwinds
of revolt" will continue to shake the foundations of the nation until justice emerges. Ignoring the
issue or hoping the movement will simply lose steam risks further dividing and destabilizing the country.

# Q5 What kind of struggle does Martin Luther King want the people to do?

King advocates for a peaceful, nonviolent, and dignified struggle. He urges his followers to
conduct their movement on the high plane of dignity and discipline, strictly avoiding physical
violence. He wants them to meet "physical force with soul force." This "creative protest" requires
immense moral strength and spiritual discipline, ensuring that their righteous thirst for freedom is not poisoned by drinking from the "cup of bitterness and hatred."
6. Should all white people be distrusted? Why not?
No, all white people should not be distrusted. King explicitly warns his followers against a
distrust of all white people, pointing out that many white brothers have come to realize that their
destiny is tied up with the destiny of Black Americans. Their freedom is inextricably bound
together. This solidarity is evidenced by the presence of many white individuals at the march,
demonstrating their shared commitment to justice and proving that the fight for civil rights requires unity, not racial division.

# Q7 When will Negros be satisfied, according to the speaker?

The Negro will never be satisfied as long as they are victims of the unspeakable horrors of police brutality. They will not be satisfied as long as they are restricted from basic public accommodations like motels and hotels, confined to ghettos, or stripped of their selfhood by "For Whites Only" signs. Furthermore, they cannot be satisfied as long as a Black person in Mississippi cannot vote and one in New York believes they have nothing to vote for. Satisfaction will only come when "justice rolls down like waters and righteousness like a mighty stream."

# Q1 Who are discriminated and how are they discriminated?

In the context of King's speech, Black Americans (historically referred to as "Negroes") are the
primary victims of discrimination. Despite the end of slavery, they faced systemic oppression
through segregation and Jim Crow laws. This discrimination permeated every aspect of life:
politically, economically, and socially. Politically, they were heavily disenfranchised and denied
the basic right to vote through violent intimidation and discriminatory laws. Economically, they
were trapped in generational poverty, living on a "lonely island of poverty" and largely excluded
from fair employment and upward mobility. Socially, they faced the daily humiliation of
segregation. They were restricted from public accommodations, forced into separate and vastly
inferior schools, and subjected to "For Whites Only" signs that stripped children of their dignity
and self-respect. Furthermore, they lived under the constant threat of "unspeakable horrors of
police brutality." King frames this multifaceted discrimination as a systemic failure of America to honor its "promissory note" of equality, reducing Black citizens to the status of marginalized
exiles within their own borders.

# Q2 Describe the speaker’s dream?

Martin Luther King Jr.’s dream is deeply rooted in the American Dream—a vision of true equality, brotherhood, and justice. He dreams of a day when the nation will rise up and live out the true meaning of its foundational creed: "We hold these truths to be self-evident, that all men
are created equal." He envisions a future of deep reconciliation, where the sons of former slaves
and the sons of former slave owners can sit together at the table of brotherhood in Georgia. He
dreams that places sweltering with the heat of injustice and oppression, like the state of
Mississippi, will transform into oases of freedom. Most famously, he dreams that his four little children will one day live in a nation where they are judged not by the color of their skin, but by
the "content of their character." His vision includes little Black boys and girls holding hands with little white boys and girls as sisters and brothers. Ultimately, it is a profound dream of spiritual and social unity, where all of God's children can join hands and sing "Free at last!"

# Q3 What kind of discrimination existed in India which is similar to racism in America? Talk about some leaders who addressed that discrimination.

A major form of discrimination in India that closely parallels the systemic racism in America is the caste system, specifically the historical practice of "untouchability." Similar to how racial
segregation in America dictated where Black people could live, learn, and eat, the caste system
dictated a rigid social hierarchy, restricting occupations and human interactions. It subjected
Dalits (historically termed "untouchables") to severe marginalization, social ostracization, and
economic deprivation. Like racism, it is a deeply entrenched social evil based entirely on birth
rather than merit. Several prominent leaders fought tirelessly against this discrimination. Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, a Dalit
himself and the chief architect of the Indian Constitution, was the most formidable voice against
caste oppression. He led massive social movements, advocated for political representation, and
ensured constitutional safeguards and affirmative action for oppressed classes. Mahatma Gandhi
also campaigned against untouchability, advocating for the entry of oppressed classes into temples
and access to public wells, utilizing nonviolent resistance much like King later would. Other
monumental figures, such as Jyotirao Phule and Periyar E. V. Ramasamy, led powerful anti-caste
movements, championed education for marginalized communities, and fought relentlessly for social equality and human dignity.
